# AI eats the world - Benedict Evans, May 2026

## Source note

This note summarizes the uploaded PDF presentation/report titled **"AI eats the world"**, credited to **Benedict Evans**, dated **May 2026**, and pointing readers to **www.ben-evans.com**. Page references below refer to the PDF page numbers. The PDF appears to be a slide deck rather than a prose report, so several claims are expressed as strategic questions, analogies, and charts rather than fully footnoted narrative arguments.

Minor metadata caveat: the title and closing pages include a visible header reading "May 2020," while the title slide, most slide footers, and deck content state **May 2026**. Page 33 also shows an "August 2026" footer. I treat **May 2026** as the report date because that is the date on the title slide and throughout most pages.

## One-sentence summary

Benedict Evans argues that generative AI is a major platform shift, but the durable value may not accrue to foundation models or raw compute; instead, the opportunity likely moves upward into applications, workflows, proprietary data, distribution, and the new kinds of questions and business models made possible when many cognitive tasks become cheap or automated.

## Executive summary

The report frames generative AI as the next major technology platform shift after mainframes, PCs, the web, and smartphones. Platform shifts reset incumbents, reallocate capital, create new gatekeepers, and force every industry to ask whether the new technology is a tool, a revenue source, or an existential threat (pp. 4-6).

The first third of the deck focuses on **capital deployment**. Evans emphasizes the extraordinary scale of AI infrastructure spending: the four major hyperscale AI/cloud platforms - Microsoft, Alphabet, AWS/Amazon, and Meta - are shown with planned 2026 capex around **$700 billion**, compared in the deck to roughly **$300 billion** for global telecoms and about **$1 trillion** for oil and gas (p. 8). This spending surge stresses the entire supply chain: Nvidia cannot satisfy demand quickly enough, TSMC capacity is constrained, semiconductor billings are spiking, semiconductor manufacturers are adding capex, and U.S. data center construction is overtaking office construction even before counting the compute equipment itself (pp. 9-12). Evans argues that these formerly asset-light internet businesses are now challenging their old financial gravity, with capex-to-sales ratios jumping sharply (p. 13). As a result, companies are reaching for structured finance, joint ventures, debt, leasing, and "other people's balance sheets," especially around OpenAI's extremely large infrastructure ambitions (pp. 14-16).

The report then asks whether this capex war produces durable strategic advantage. Evans compares LLM capacity to mobile networks in 2010: a scarce, costly, metered resource facing explosive usage growth, opaque marginal cost, and pricing models that have not yet reached equilibrium (pp. 20-24). He argues that current pricing power may be a temporary effect of supply-demand imbalance. Over time, raw models may look more like commodity infrastructure: capital-intensive, lacking strong network effects, and potentially low-margin (pp. 24-27). His provisional thesis is deliberately cautious: chat is a poor universal interface, general AI use needs applications, labs cannot build every application, models may commoditize, and innovation may move up the stack (p. 27).

The second third of the deck focuses on **deployment**. ChatGPT is shown as having enormous reach - over 900 million weekly users, with only about 5% paying - but Evans argues that consumer adoption is still "a mile wide and an inch deep" because many users experiment rather than rely on it daily (pp. 29-31). Workplace use is growing rapidly but unevenly: many workers have used AI, fewer use it every day, and there is a "capacity gap" between occasional use and deep organizational deployment (p. 32). Evans uses the spreadsheet analogy to show that some roles immediately see the killer app, while others see promise but need much more productization (p. 33).

Evans describes technology deployment as a three-stage pattern: first **absorb** the new tool into old workflows, then **innovate** into things that were newly possible, and eventually **disrupt** by redefining the question (p. 34). Early AI use cases are therefore concentrated in analytics, productivity, marketing, customer support, back-office processes, and especially coding (pp. 35-37). However, he stresses that writing code itself is not usually the hard part. The harder problems are deciding what the software should do, how it fits the market, which workflows it changes, and what should be bundled or unbundled across horizontal systems, vertical software, custom apps, spreadsheets, email, and informal processes (pp. 40-44).

The deployment section also argues that AI software is not simply "a model plus a prompt." It needs use-case discovery, interfaces, tooling, process redesign, go-to-market motion, proprietary data, workflow integration, and often systems integrators or consultants (pp. 44-46). Enterprise AI adoption is therefore full of pilots but slower production use, especially outside software and customer service (p. 47). Startups are expected to unbundle old use cases from Google, Excel, email, Oracle, and perhaps even ChatGPT itself (p. 48).

The final third focuses on **change**. Evans does not present a deterministic forecast; instead, he argues that AI questions usually have two useful answers: "no one knows" and "what happened the last time everything changed?" (pp. 50-51, 73-74). AI adds probabilistic systems on top of deterministic systems and expands automation from tasks describable in logical steps to tasks with enough training data or scalable verification (p. 52).

The report's core labor and business-model question is whether AI automates a discrete task, changes a job, destroys a cost-base moat, or enables entirely new demand. Elevator attendants are used as an example of a job that was largely reduced to a button, while accountants are used as a counterexample where automation changed the work and may have increased demand through price elasticity and new scope (pp. 54-56). The barcode and supermarket SKU example shows that when a task becomes cheap - inventory management - companies may not simply reduce labor; they may expand complexity and choice (p. 57). The internet example shows that removing distribution costs can destroy industry moats based on physical media, printing, cables, or stores (p. 58). The AI equivalent is to ask which industries rely on "boring" cognitive work that AI can reduce toward zero (p. 59).

Evans then explores AI as "infinite interns": not only one assistant doing a task, but a million assistants operating across enormous volumes of content, calls, transactions, products, or user behavior (pp. 60-61). This matters because AI may convert previously unstructured information into searchable, analyzable, actionable knowledge. Examples include listening to every customer call, understanding what products actually are and why users buy them, moving recommendations beyond correlation toward semantic understanding, and asking business questions that deterministic systems could not answer (pp. 61-66).

The conclusion is deliberately open-ended. Evans says the useful task is not to predict exactly what AI will change, but to identify which tasks are being commoditized, which jobs are really bundles of implicit judgment and taste, which moats depend on automatable cost bases, what competitors can now do, and what business models might be unlocked or broken. The final advice is to presume radical uncertainty while asking sharper strategic questions (pp. 67-78).

## Key claims and arguments

### 1. Generative AI is a platform shift, not just a product category

Evans places generative AI in a sequence of major technology shifts: mainframes, PCs, web, smartphones, and now generative AI (p. 4). The core claim is that a platform shift changes where innovation, investment, company creation, gatekeeping, and value capture occur (p. 5). The chart of Microsoft's operating-system share of global computer unit sales is used to show that even dominant platform-era winners can be reset when the next platform emerges (p. 6).

### 2. AI demand is triggering a capital deployment shock

The deck's capital section argues that AI is producing a sudden, enormous increase in infrastructure spending. Hyperscaler capex is shown accelerating sharply from 2010 to 2026 guidance, with the big four planning around $700B of 2026 capex (p. 8). This demand then cascades into Nvidia revenue, TSMC constraints, semiconductor billings, semiconductor capex, and data center construction (pp. 9-12).

### 3. Hyperscalers are becoming less asset-light

Evans argues that Meta, Microsoft, Alphabet, and Amazon historically funded capex from free cash flow and were perceived as relatively asset-light software or internet businesses. AI infrastructure pushes capex/sales ratios much higher and changes the financial profile of these firms (p. 13). This motivates structured financing, joint ventures, debt, and balance-sheet engineering (p. 14).

### 4. OpenAI's infrastructure ambitions are financially unusual

OpenAI is framed as trying to join the infrastructure club without having a hyperscaler's balance sheet. Evans cites plans and aspirations such as 30GW+ of capacity, possible figures of $1.4T or $600B by 2030, and a hypothetical 1GW/week construction rate at $20B/GW, implying roughly $1T annually (p. 15). His language emphasizes "other people's balance sheets," circular revenue, and operational plate-spinning (p. 15).

### 5. Supply bottlenecks are broad, not limited to GPUs

The deck says deployment hits bottlenecks across GPUs, memory, multi-year power backlogs, and community or political backlash, with China noted as an exception in the power-backlog context (p. 16). Kevin Scott's quote on the difficulty of building capacity since ChatGPT reinforces the supply-constrained story (p. 17).

### 6. AI-lab growth is extraordinary but business models are unstable

Evans shows reported monthly or annualized revenue growth for OpenAI and Anthropic rising extremely quickly, but he explicitly caveats that these are press-reported round numbers and that business models are not near equilibrium (p. 18). The following slides ask how long usage growth can outpace efficiency gains, how much the industry will spend chasing frontier models, and how price, usage, and capex can equilibrate (pp. 19-20).

### 7. LLM pricing may resemble mobile-network pricing more than SaaS pricing

The report draws an analogy between mobile networks around 2010 and LLMs in 2026: bits are like tokens, marginal costs matter, capacity is limited, and pricing initially fails to align use cases, value, and capacity (pp. 21-22). In mobile, flat-rate capped bundles and Wi-Fi/edge offload helped reach equilibrium. Evans suggests AI labs may be able to price for ROI during scarcity, but long-term pricing power is uncertain (p. 22).

### 8. Models may commoditize

Evans argues that commodity infrastructure often does not capture the value created up the stack. Mobile networks carry enormous value but other companies build the services and capture much of the use-case value (p. 24). He then shows benchmark convergence among frontier LLMs and notes that for most general or consumer uses, models look similar and lack obvious network effects (p. 25). The strategic question is whether winning the capex war yields high-margin software-like economics or low-margin commodity-infrastructure economics (p. 26).

### 9. The provisional thesis: innovation moves up the stack

The deck's provisional thesis is that chat is a poor general UX, general use requires applications, labs cannot build or generate all applications, models may have no network effects, models may become infrastructure, and innovation may move up the stack (p. 27). This is one of the deck's central takeaways.

### 10. Adoption is broad, but depth is uneven

Evans notes that ChatGPT has over 900M weekly active users, but only around 5% are paying (p. 29). He interprets user-reported 2025 prompt volumes as evidence that many users are experimenting rather than depending on ChatGPT daily (p. 30). U.S. consumer surveys show many people use AI sometimes, but fewer use it daily (p. 31). Workplace surveys show a similar pattern: lots of exposure, less deep use (p. 32).

### 11. AI deployment follows the old pattern: absorb, innovate, disrupt

New technologies are first used to do old things better, then to do new things that only the technology makes possible, and eventually to redefine the question (p. 34). Current AI use cases mainly automate obvious work: productivity, insights, customer service, marketing, support, back-office processes, and coding (pp. 35-37).

### 12. Coding is transformed, but code generation is not the whole problem

The deck treats coding as the leading enterprise AI spending category (p. 37) and uses Zuckerberg's quote to emphasize that small teams can now build quickly (p. 38). But Evans counters that writing code is not the hardest part: teams still need to know what the code should do, where it fits in the market, and how it relates to existing workflows, systems of record, custom apps, spreadsheets, and informal processes (pp. 40-42).

### 13. Building AI software requires productization, not just models

Evans asks how to turn an amazing raw technology into usable software (p. 44). The answer includes use cases, tooling, graphical interfaces, processes, go-to-market, proprietary data, and workflows. He asks whether the model belongs at the top of the stack or the bottom (p. 44). Accenture's rising generative AI contract value and consultancy partnerships reinforce the idea that automation still requires human organizational work (pp. 45-46).

### 14. Enterprise AI is in the pilot-heavy phase

Bain data show high development/pilot adoption across enterprise use cases but lower production deployment, especially beyond software and customer service (p. 47). This supports Evans's view that enterprise deployment takes time and often begins with disappointment or complexity before production value appears.

### 15. Startups will unbundle use cases

Y Combinator data are used to show a wave of AI startups. Evans expects startups to unbundle use cases from Google, Excel, email, Oracle, and perhaps ChatGPT itself (p. 48). This aligns with the thesis that value may shift to applications and workflows rather than remain at the model layer.

### 16. Forecasting is hard; historical analogy is more useful than prediction

The change section opens with the difficulty of prediction and asks the reader to imagine forecasting the internet's effects in 1997 (pp. 50-51). Later, Evans says AI questions tend to have two answers: no one knows, or what happened the last time everything changed (p. 73). The report treats uncertainty as the core strategic condition.

### 17. AI expands the boundary of automation

Traditional automation worked when tasks could be described in logical steps. AI enables automation where there is enough training data or where verification is scalable (p. 52). This is a key conceptual distinction: AI does not merely make existing software better; it changes what kinds of work can be encoded into systems.

### 18. Automation can eliminate jobs, change jobs, or create demand

The elevator-attendant example shows a job that effectively became a button after automatic elevators (p. 54). The accounting examples show a more complex story: automation can make some tasks cheaper while expanding the profession's scope or demand (pp. 55-56). Evans is warning against one-dimensional labor forecasts.

### 19. If a task becomes cheap, companies may do more of it

The supermarket SKU chart shows that automated inventory management enabled grocery stores to carry far more products (p. 57). The implication for AI is that making a cognitive task cheap may not simply remove cost; it may allow much more complexity, personalization, monitoring, analysis, or choice.

### 20. AI can attack cost-base moats

The internet removed distribution costs that protected music, journalism, TV, and retail from competition (p. 58). Evans asks what industries are protected by a cognitive or administrative cost base that AI can automate toward zero (p. 59). This is one of the report's most important strategic questions for incumbents.

### 21. "Infinite interns" changes what questions are possible

The report uses the phrase "AI gives you infinite interns" and asks what changes if you have a million interns or one intern a million times faster (pp. 60-61). The point is not just cheaper task execution but new scale: listening to every call, scanning every item, understanding every product, summarizing every customer interaction, and detecting patterns humans could not inspect manually.

### 22. Search, recommendations, and commerce may shift from correlation to semantic understanding

Evans argues that Amazon, Google, and Meta often know correlations around behavior but not necessarily what products are or why people buy them (p. 62). He uses Zuckerberg's quote to describe a future where systems build a more first-principles understanding of content and user interests (p. 63). This opens new questions in shopping, discovery, personalization, and agentic purchasing (p. 64). Bain consumer-search data are included as a reality check that generative search is still early and may be additive rather than purely substitutive (p. 65).

### 23. The key distinction is task versus job

Evans repeatedly asks what the user actually wants and how to split the task from the job (pp. 67-68). Tasks include getting SKUs, writing code, making spreadsheets, or drafting specs. Jobs include implicit knowledge, opinion, taste, new ideas, and judgment about what is different rather than average (pp. 67-68). This distinction matters for labor, software design, and strategy.

### 24. Outcomes will vary by industry

The Uber/taxi and Airbnb/hotel comparison shows that software sometimes displaces incumbents and sometimes remains a smaller complement (p. 75). The Philippine IT-BPM example asks whether outsourcing work based on skill/income arbitrage will be a job or a task when AI enters the picture (p. 76). The recorded-music example asks whether the bigger change is removing the need to buy a CD or inventing access to all music (p. 77).

### 25. The final recommendation is strategic humility plus better questions

Evans ends with three strategic questions: what can you and competitors do with AI, can AI unlock or break something crucial in your business model, and how do you operate under radical uncertainty (p. 78). The deck's practical value is a framework for asking these questions, not a fixed prediction of the future.

## Implications by audience

### For AI labs and foundation-model companies

The report implies that labs face a race that is both technically and financially brutal. Scaling requires vast capital, chips, power, data centers, and financing structures. But the end state may not reward all that capital with software-like margins if models converge, open source pressures pricing, edge inference emerges, or applications capture most of the value.

### For hyperscalers

Hyperscalers may be the only actors with sufficient cash flow, balance sheets, and infrastructure experience to fund the current race. However, Evans suggests that AI capex is changing their financial profile and creating risk around asset lives, utilization, efficiency gains, and future pricing.

### For enterprises

Enterprises should not expect AI adoption to be solved by buying a model subscription. They need use-case discovery, workflow redesign, data strategy, governance, integration, and measurement. Early wins will likely come from obvious productivity, support, analytics, back-office, and coding use cases, but deeper transformation depends on productized workflows.

### For startups

Startups should look for specific use cases currently trapped inside broad horizontal tools, messy workflows, or incumbent systems. The opportunity is to unbundle the job-to-be-done from Google, Excel, email, Oracle, Salesforce, ChatGPT, or internal bespoke processes and rebuild it around AI-native assumptions.

### For incumbents outside tech

The strategic risk is not "AI exists" but whether AI makes a core cost base, workflow, service layer, distribution channel, or labor-arbitrage model much cheaper for competitors. Incumbents should identify which "boring" work is essential to their moat and whether it can now be automated.

### For workers and labor-market analysts

Evans's framework suggests avoiding binary claims that AI will either replace all jobs or simply augment all jobs. The sharper question is whether a job is mostly one automatable task, a changing bundle of tasks, or a role whose value is judgment, taste, accountability, context, and tacit knowledge.

## Atomic notes

### AI capex may not guarantee model-layer value capture

AI infrastructure spending is unprecedented, but Evans warns that the winners of the infrastructure buildout may not capture the most attractive economics if models become commodity infrastructure and applications capture the value (pp. 8, 24-27).

### Scarcity creates pricing power, but equilibrium may remove it

AI labs can charge based on ROI while compute is scarce, but mobile-network history suggests that long-term pricing tends to align around capacity, bundles, offload, competition, and user expectations (pp. 21-24).

### ChatGPT usage is massive but not yet necessarily habitual

The report treats ChatGPT's 900M+ weekly users as evidence of extraordinary reach, while the low paid share and prompt-frequency data suggest that many users are still experimenting rather than treating it as essential infrastructure (pp. 29-31).

### Coding is the first obvious enterprise AI wedge

Enterprise AI spending data show coding far ahead of other categories, but Evans argues that code generation still leaves the harder work of product definition, workflow design, business context, and market fit (pp. 37-42).

### AI deployment is a services problem as much as a software problem

Rising consulting contracts and enterprise pilots suggest that turning AI into value requires discovery, change management, workflow integration, and manual organizational work (pp. 44-47).

### Tasks are easier to automate than jobs

Evans's labor-market analogies show that some roles collapse when a core task disappears, while others expand or transform because cheaper task execution enables more work, more complexity, or new services (pp. 54-57, 67-68).

### The most strategic AI questions concern moats

The internet destroyed moats based on physical distribution costs. AI may attack moats based on administrative, analytical, clerical, service, or knowledge-work cost bases (pp. 58-59).

### "Infinite interns" means scale, not just assistance

The important shift is not that AI writes one email or summarizes one call. It is that AI can inspect every call, every SKU, every customer interaction, and every document, changing what questions organizations can ask (pp. 60-66).

### Strategic uncertainty is the point

The report's final position is that no one knows the precise outcome. The best response is to ask historically informed questions about capability, competition, business-model exposure, and uncertainty (pp. 73-78).
